How Much Does It Cost to Move to a New City After College?

Moving after graduation costs more than most new graduates expect, and the biggest costs arrive before your first paycheck does. This guide breaks the move into the one-time costs you pay up front, the costs of your first month, and the simple choices that bring the total down. The numbers below are examples to help you plan. Prices vary a lot by city, so replace them with real quotes before you commit.

Start with the cost you can't avoid: getting into an apartment

For most graduates, the largest bill is the cost of signing a lease. Landlords often ask for the first month's rent and a security deposit on the day you sign, and many add an application fee, a pet fee, or a move-in fee. A common rule of thumb is that you should be ready to pay at least two months of rent before you receive a single paycheck. If a place asks for much more than that, ask what each charge covers and which ones are refundable.

One-time moving costs

How you get your belongings to the new city changes the price more than anything else. A rental truck gives you control but adds fuel, insurance, and sometimes mileage fees. Hiring movers costs more but saves time and effort. Shipping boxes or using a moving container sits in the middle. If you are moving with only a car's worth of belongings, driving yourself is usually the cheapest option.

Do not forget the smaller items: packing tape and boxes, a few cleaning supplies, and basic kitchen and bedding items if you are leaving a furnished dorm or shared house. These add up quickly when you buy them all in one weekend.

Your first month after you arrive

Once you are in, a second round of costs begins. Utilities often need a deposit or a setup fee, and internet service may charge for installation. You will also buy groceries, a transit pass or parking, and probably a few things you forgot. Plan for your first month to cost more than a normal month, and keep a small cushion for surprises.

Ways to bring the cost down

  • Compare at least three quotes for trucks, movers, or shipping, and book early for end-of-month dates, which are the most expensive.
  • Sell or give away large items instead of moving them. A cheap replacement can cost less than the fuel and truck space.
  • Ask your employer whether they offer relocation help or a signing bonus. Many first jobs do not, but it costs nothing to ask.
  • Look for apartments that include utilities, or split costs with a roommate to cut the deposit and monthly rent.
  • Move in the middle of the month if you can. Prices and demand are usually lower than at the start or end.
  • Keep every receipt. Some job-related moving costs may matter at tax time, so check current rules or ask a tax professional.
Not sure what to do first? Write your own version of the table above using real quotes, then add a cushion of at least 10 percent. If the total is more than you have saved, you can move later, choose a cheaper way to move, find a roommate, or ask your new employer about help.
